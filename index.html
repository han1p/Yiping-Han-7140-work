Integrated Research and Design Proposal

Common Table

Helping international students and low-income residents find community food support without confusion, stigma, or overload.

Context Research Principles Direction Commitments

Design context

Food relief is available, but finding the right help often requires time, confidence, and local knowledge that many people do not have.

This proposal investigates how a digital service could make community food support more discoverable, understandable, and dignified for people navigating cost-of-living pressure in an unfamiliar system.

Project framing

  • Primary community International students, new arrivals, and residents with limited disposable income
  • Service concept A mobile-first directory and planning tool for food banks, community kitchens, campus support, and low-cost groceries
  • Core design question How might we reduce friction and stigma when people need immediate, practical food support?

1. Context and problem space

Why this matters

Observed challenge

Support options are fragmented across campus pages, charities, local councils, social media posts, and word of mouth. People in urgent need must compare opening hours, eligibility rules, travel distance, and required documents while under stress.

Design opportunity

A service can reduce that burden by translating scattered information into plain-language guidance, accessible journey planning, and trust-building explanations that respect cultural difference and personal dignity.

Context

Cost pressure changes behaviour

People delay seeking help, ration meals, or rely on inconsistent advice from peers before approaching formal services.

Barrier

Information is difficult to interpret

Eligibility terms, map distance, transport complexity, and availability windows are often unclear or outdated.

Impact

Stress compounds exclusion

Users with low digital confidence, limited English, disability, or stigma concerns are most likely to drop off.

2. Research synthesis

What the investigation suggests

Method 01

Secondary research scan

Reviewed recurring themes from public discussions around food insecurity, student hardship, service access, and digital exclusion.

Used here to identify patterns, not to claim completed fieldwork.

Method 02

Service precedent review

Compared charity directories, university support pages, transport-enabled maps, and crisis-support interfaces for clarity and trust signals.

Method 03

Journey and edge-case analysis

Mapped the steps from “I need help today” to “I arrive at a service prepared,” including uncertainty, shame, and access constraints.

Key insight themes

  1. Urgent users need “what can I do today?” answers before they want background information.
  2. Trust grows when services explain practical details such as cost, ID requirements, transport, and cultural food suitability.
  3. People value discretion; language that sounds charitable or corrective can discourage engagement.
  4. Accessibility is not a separate layer because low bandwidth, translation needs, and cognitive load affect core usability.

Design implications

  • Prioritise immediate action paths over deep menu structures.
  • Use filters based on user tasks rather than organisational categories.
  • Explain support in neutral, respectful language.
  • Show “what to expect” before asking users to travel.

3. Users and needs

Who we are designing for

Mina, 22

International student living off casual shifts

Mina needs affordable food this week, but she is unsure which services welcome students and whether she will be judged for asking.

Need: Fast reassurance, clear eligibility, and transport-aware options near campus.

David, 48

Resident with chronic pain and limited mobility

David can access support only if locations are physically reachable and the page clearly states accessibility, queue expectations, and seating.

Need: Reliable access information and fewer cognitive steps.

Asha, 31

New parent navigating a new suburb

Asha wants options that match childcare timing, dietary preferences, and family quantity without reading long blocks of institutional text.

Need: Plain-language summaries, family suitability, and culturally sensitive food indicators.

4. Precedents and constraints

What existing services taught the project

Directories

Strength: breadth

They list many services, but often depend on dense text and unclear update cycles.

Takeaway: breadth must be paired with confidence signals and recency.

Institutional support pages

Strength: authority

They are trusted, but usually organised by provider structure rather than user urgency.

Takeaway: authority should be combined with task-first navigation.

Maps and delivery apps

Strength: practicality

They make distance and route friction visible, which is essential for time-sensitive decisions.

Takeaway: practical logistics are part of the service, not a secondary feature.

Constraint

Low trust in outdated information

Constraint

Mixed digital literacy

Constraint

Sensitive personal context

Constraint

Different language and dietary needs

5. Design principles

The logic connecting insight to action

Immediate clarity

Because users may arrive stressed or hungry, the interface should surface urgent next steps within the first screen.

Dignified language

Because stigma can deter use, microcopy should avoid moralising, charity-first framing, and unnecessary personal disclosure.

Practical accessibility

Because usability depends on context, accessibility must include screen-reader support, contrast, translation readiness, and low-bandwidth resilience.

Cultural awareness

Because food has cultural, religious, and family significance, the system should show suitability cues instead of assuming one universal standard.

6. Proposed design direction

What the A3 implementation should become

Core experience

A mobile-first platform that asks a few respectful, task-based questions such as “Need food today?”, “Can you travel?”, and “Do you need halal, vegetarian, or family-sized options?” It then recommends suitable services with plain-language preparation steps.

Information architecture

  • Today / This week / Ongoing support pathways
  • Map and list views with transport time
  • Service detail cards with eligibility, cost, food type, and accessibility notes
  • Preparation checklist for what to bring and what to expect
01

Entry

Calm landing screen with urgency-based choices, no forced sign-up, and language selection visible immediately.

02

Match

Filter results by location, transport, dietary preferences, cost, and accessibility support.

03

Prepare

Each result explains wait times, arrival instructions, accepted referrals, and quieter alternatives.

7. Accessibility, ethics, and culture

Non-negotiable commitments

Accessibility

  • WCAG-aligned contrast, semantic HTML, visible focus, and keyboard navigation
  • Short paragraphs, clear headings, and reduced cognitive load
  • Responsive layout that remains usable on older phones and limited data

Ethics

  • Collect the minimum data needed and avoid profiling people based on hardship
  • Make uncertainty visible when service details cannot be confirmed
  • Design for dignity by treating support access as normal and practical

Cultural considerations

  • Use inclusive imagery and translation-ready content structure
  • Signal dietary and faith-related suitability where possible
  • Recognise that family, community, and privacy expectations differ across users

8. Outcome

Summary of the proposal

This website demonstrates a clear line from research to design action: fragmented support systems create avoidable stress, so the proposed interface responds with urgency-based navigation, respectful language, practical logistics, and culturally aware access cues.

The project is not only about finding food services more efficiently. It is about designing a system that reduces uncertainty, protects dignity, and acknowledges accessibility and ethics as central design requirements from the start.
